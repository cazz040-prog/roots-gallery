The Craft

Made by Hand.
Carried with Intention.

Each Namji doll begins as a single piece of hardwood, shaped by hand tools passed between generations. Beads are threaded one by one. Cowrie shells are knotted and layered. Metal is hammered and bent. There is no shortcut in this work — only time, skill, and the weight of tradition.

Cultural Context

From the Namji People of Cameroon

The Namji — also known as the Namchi or Dowayo people — inhabit the Faro and Déo Division of the Adamawa Region in north-central Cameroon. Their handcrafted dolls are among the most recognised objects of Central African material culture.

Traditionally, a Namji doll was given to a young woman as she prepared for marriage and motherhood. She would carry it, care for it, and adorn it further over time — adding her own beads, shells, or cloth. The doll accumulated history, becoming a layered document of a life.

The dolls are associated with fertility, prosperity, good fortune, and ancestral protection. They are not considered sacred in a religious sense, but they are deeply meaningful — objects that hold intention, and that pass that intention forward.
